**Title:** Has anyone else noticed guests remember the smell of a dinner long after they forget the food?

Last October I hosted a twelve-person dinner in a Brooklyn carriage house. About an hour before doors, I had a baker slide a tray of fig leaves into a 300 degree oven, purely to warm them. No candles, no diffuser, nothing anyone would consciously clock.

Nobody mentioned the leaves all night. But when one guest wrote to me in January, she didn't talk about the menu or the wine. She described the room, and the warmth of it, and that something green and slightly sweet was in the air when she walked in.

That's stuck with me, because scent is the sense most tied to memory, and it's the one most hosts leave completely to chance. Usually it's whatever the kitchen happens to be producing, or a candle bought five minutes before guests arrive, which often fights with the food.

A few things I've learned since: keep it subtle enough that nobody can name it, use natural sources that suit the menu rather than fighting it, and decide what the entry moment smells like deliberately, because that's the first impression.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How can I use scent to make a dinner party more memorable?

Scent is the sense most closely tied to memory, which makes it the most underused tool a host has. Most people leave it to chance: whatever the kitchen produces, or a candle lit at the last minute. If you want guests to remember the evening, treat scent as a design decision.

Here is what I have learned curating private dinners.

First, keep it subtle. The best scent is one guests can't quite name. If someone says 'what is that smell?', it is too loud. You want a background warmth that makes the room feel considered.

Second, use natural, edible-adjacent sources. Last October I seated twelve people in a Brooklyn carriage house, and an hour before the doors opened I had a baker warm a tray of fig leaves in a 300 degree oven. Nobody mentioned them all night. But when one guest wrote to me in January, she described the feel of the room rather than the menu. That is the effect you are after.

Third, match the scent to the menu. Heavy perfumed candles fight with food, and they dull the first bite. Herbs, citrus peel, warmed leaves, wood, or bread work because they sit in the same world as what is being served.

Fourth, plan the entry moment. The first breath when a guest walks in sets the emotional tone, so decide what it smells like, and make sure it is different from the dining table itself.

Finally, air the space beforehand, so you are adding one clear note instead of layering over stale air.

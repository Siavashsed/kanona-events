**Title:** Has anyone else found that where guests first stop matters more than the icebreakers?

Something I keep learning again: guests don't really decide whether to talk to strangers. The room decides for them, usually before they've taken their coat off.

Last October I ran a supper for 64 people in an old rope works in Leith. The bar ended up about eleven feet from the entrance. For the first fifty minutes almost nobody spoke to anyone they hadn't arrived with. Couples collected their wine and stayed locked onto each other, because the first natural pause point was also the most crowded and exposed spot in the room.

My takeaway is that the first place a guest stops matters more than the menu, the playlist or any icebreaker. If that pause happens in a pinch point by the door, people protect their space instead of opening up. If it happens deeper in the room, with guests side by side rather than face to face and somewhere to rest a glass, conversation tends to start on its own.

Do others plan the first few metres of a venue this deliberately? Or have you found other fixes that work?

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How does the layout of an event venue affect whether guests talk to each other?

More than almost anything else you control. After years of curating experiential events, I've come to believe guests rarely choose whether to mingle. The room makes that choice for them, usually in the first few minutes.

The most important moment is the first pause. Every guest arrives, gets their bearings and stops somewhere, usually to collect a drink. Where that stop happens shapes the rest of the night. If it is a crowded pinch point near the entrance, people go defensive. They hold onto whoever they came with, keep their backs to the wall and wait for someone else to make the first move.

I learned this the hard way. In October 2024 I curated a supper for 64 people in a former rope works in Leith, and the bar sat just eleven feet from the door. For the first fifty minutes almost nobody spoke to a stranger. Nothing was wrong with the guests or the food. The layout had put the first pause in the worst possible place. I wrote up the full story and what I changed afterwards here: https://kanonaprojects.com/spatial-flow-guides-conversation-events/

A few principles I now use on every project:

1. Put the first stop deeper into the room. Give people a few steps of walking to settle before they have to stand still.

2. Design for side by side, not face to face. Standing shoulder to shoulder at a ledge or a long counter makes a comment to a stranger feel easy. Squaring up to someone feels like an interview.

3. Give hands something to do. A surface to rest a glass on keeps people in place long enough for a conversation to start.

4. Control the density. Too sparse feels exposed and too tight feels hostile. You want enough closeness that a brush of the elbow becomes an excuse to talk.

Plan the flow first. The conversation follows.

**Title:** Did Travis Scott's 2020 Fortnite show actually change how any of you design live experiences?

I've curated live events for years, and I keep coming back to Travis Scott's Astronomical show in Fortnite from April 2020. Epic reported 12.3 million concurrent players at the first show, and roughly 27.7 million unique players across the run. Every venue I'd worked in was dark that week.

The number isn't what stuck with me. It was how the show was built around things physical venues can't do. The ground dropped away, players were pulled underwater, then launched into space. Nobody had a bad seat because there was no stage to face. The artist was the architecture.

It also skipped things we take for granted: no crowd noise, no shared heat, no stranger singing off key next to you. It ran about nine minutes, and it felt more complete than most two hour sets I've stood through.

For those of you in live production: did this shift how you pitch immersive or hybrid shows, or did it feel like a pandemic one-off? I'm curious whether anyone has actually borrowed the pacing or the idea of moving the audience instead of the lights.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** What made Travis Scott's Fortnite concert so successful, and what can live event designers learn from it?

I'm an experiential event curator, so I watched Astronomical in April 2020 with a professional itch. Every venue I'd worked in was closed at the time. A few things made it work.

1. Scale with zero friction. Epic reported 12.3 million concurrent players at the first show and about 27.7 million unique players across the scheduled runs. There were no tickets, no travel and no queue. The audience was already inside the venue before the show was announced.

2. Repeat showings across time zones. Five showings over three days meant almost nobody had to stay up until 4 a.m. That sounds like logistics, but it's really hospitality.

3. The environment was the performance. There was no stage with a crowd facing it. Instead the whole map transformed. Players were dropped underwater, flung into space, and watched Scott grow to the size of a skyscraper. Nobody had a bad seat because there were no seats.

4. Brevity. It ran about nine minutes and ended while people still wanted more, which is the hardest discipline in live production.

5. A reason to be there live. It premiered a new track, The Scotts with Kid Cudi, so showing up felt like witnessing something rather than streaming it.

For physical events, point three is the lesson that carries over. Stop thinking of the room as a box that holds a show. Design the room to change around the audience with reveals, shifting levels, and light that moves attention.

What it couldn't replicate was the body in the crowd: the heat, the stranger singing off key beside you. That's still the reason to be in the room. I wrote a longer piece about it, including watching it on my nephew's PS4: https://kanonaprojects.com/travis-scott-astronomical-fortnite-2020/

The best virtual shows don't replace live events. They raise the bar for how deliberately we design them.

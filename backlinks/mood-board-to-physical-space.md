**Title:** Does anyone else do a sensory walkthrough before trusting the mood board?

Something I keep relearning: a mood board is a visual document, but a room is not a visual experience. A picture has no temperature and no echo.

A while back I pinned forty-one Hammershøi interiors to a board for a twelve-person supper in an old print shop. Pewter light, bare boards, one chair against a wall. It looked right on screen. The board couldn't show me what bare floors and hard walls do to twelve people talking at once, how a big industrial space holds heat in March, or what an old print shop smells like before you touch it.

Now I rebuild the board in the senses it leaves out before I commit to anything:

- Sound: clap in the empty space, then picture it full. Hard surfaces turn conversation into noise fast.
- Temperature: visit at the same hour and season as the event.
- Smell: note what the room already smells like and decide whether you're working with it or against it.
- Touch: what guests will actually sit on, hold and lean on.

I'm curious whether other planners do something like this or mostly go by the visuals.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How do you turn a mood board into a real event space that actually feels the way it looks?

Start by accepting what the mood board can't do. A mood board only shows how things look. A room is felt through sound, temperature, smell and touch, and a picture carries none of those. If you build straight from the images, you can end up with a space that photographs well and feels wrong.

I learned this on a project in March 2023. I had pinned forty-one Hammershøi interiors to a board for a twelve-person supper in a former print shop in Leslieville. Pewter light, bare boards, a single chair against a wall. The visual direction was settled. What the board couldn't tell me was how those hard surfaces would sound with twelve people talking, how the space would hold heat, or what an old print shop smells like.

So my first step now is to rebuild the board in the senses it leaves out:

Sound. Stand in the empty venue and clap. Bare floors and plaster walls amplify everything. Decide early whether you need textiles, rugs or seating arrangements that soften it.

Temperature. Visit at the same time of day and year as the event. A cool, sparse look can feel serene in a photo and simply cold in person.

Scent. Every venue already smells of something. Choose whether to work with it, neutralize it or layer over it, and don't leave it to chance.

Touch. List what guests will physically handle: chairs, napkins, glassware, the table edge. Those materials do as much for the mood as the lighting does.

Only after that do I translate the visuals into specific objects and light levels. The images set the direction, and the other senses decide whether the room works.

**Title:** Does the invitation do more for an evening than the room does? A supper in a Lisbon print shop made me think so

I curate small experiential events, and one lesson keeps repeating: guests start deciding what the night will be the moment the invitation lands, not when they walk in.

The example that taught me this was a supper for fourteen in a disused print shop in Lisbon, back in March 2022. The invitation was a letterpress card on cream stock. No street address, just coordinates, and one line: arrive hungry, leave transformed. Nothing else was explained.

By the time people arrived, they had already spent days wondering what it would be. They had talked about it with friends and decided what to wear. They showed up curious and slightly nervous in a good way, and that did more for the atmosphere than any lighting or music choice I made that night.

The practical takeaway for me is that restraint in the invitation works. Tell people less, but make what you do say specific and tactile. A vague 'join us for an evening' creates no anticipation. A precise, odd detail does.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How much does the invitation affect how guests experience an event?

More than most hosts expect. In my experience as an experiential event curator, the invitation is the first moment of the event, not a logistics notice that comes before it.

Here is why. Expectation is built by guests, not delivered by hosts. From the moment an invitation lands, people start imagining the evening. They guess at the venue, the other guests and the food, and they decide how to dress and how much to look forward to it. By the time they arrive, a good share of the night has already been decided in their heads. Your job is to shape that anticipation, not to start from zero at the door.

I learned this at a supper for fourteen in a disused print shop in Lisbon in March 2022. The invitation was a letterpress card on cream stock. It gave coordinates instead of an address and one line: arrive hungry, leave transformed. That was all. Guests arrived curious, a little nervous and fully engaged, and the room barely had to work to win them over.

A few principles I now follow:

1. Be specific rather than vague. One odd, concrete detail creates more anticipation than a paragraph of description.
2. Withhold deliberately. Leaving gaps invites guests to fill them with their own imagination, which is usually more compelling than anything you could state.
3. Make it physical when you can. Paper stock, printing and the way it arrives all signal what kind of evening to expect.
4. Keep your promise. Intrigue only works if the night delivers on the tone you set.

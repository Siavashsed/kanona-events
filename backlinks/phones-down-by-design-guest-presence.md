**Title:** Has anyone else found that phone bans at events backfire, and that the room itself does more than any rule?

I've been running supper events for a while, and the phone question comes up all the time. Hosts either ignore it or go fully phone-free with pouches and signs. In my experience the policing version kills the mood faster than the phones ever did.

What's worked better for me is making the phone an afterthought instead of a rule. Last March I hosted forty-two people in an old print works in Hackney. By the door there was a single linen-lined basket, presented as part of the coat check, not as a confiscation. No sign, no speech. Phones went in with the coats, and almost nobody came back for theirs until they were leaving.

The basket wasn't really why it worked, though. The room had to be worth paying attention to: low light, things happening at the table, and pacing that never left ten dead minutes where people reach for their pocket out of habit.

I'd like to hear whether others have tried something similar, or found the opposite, that guests resent any nudge at all.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How do you get guests to stop using their phones at a dinner party or event without being rude?

I design events for a living, and the honest answer is that you mostly can't make them, and trying usually backfires. Phone bans, pouches and announcements turn you into the room's enforcer. Guests go along with it resentfully, sneak a look in the loo, and the evening starts to feel a bit like a school trip.

What works better is removing the reasons people reach for a phone in the first place. Here's what I rely on:

1. Make putting the phone away a gesture, not a rule. At a supper I ran for forty-two people in a former print works in Hackney, I put one linen-lined basket by the door as part of the coat check. No sign, no instruction. Phones went in with the coats, and nobody came back for theirs until they were heading home.

2. Get rid of the dead air. People pick up their phones in the gaps: waiting for a course, standing alone with a drink, the lull after arriving. Plan the first twenty minutes carefully, give people something to do with their hands, and keep the time between moments short.

3. Give the eye somewhere better to go. Low, warm light, something happening at the table, a room that rewards looking around. Phones win in a flat, bright, static space.

4. Seat people for conversation. Mix groups on purpose and keep tables small enough that nobody is stranded at one end.

5. Don't moralise. If someone photographs the main course, let them. A host scanning the room for rule-breakers is more distracting than any screen.
